# 50 Cold DM Scripts for LinkedIn B2B

> A practical collection of 50 LinkedIn cold DM scripts for B2B sales teams, founders, and anyone starting prospect conversations.

LinkedIn cold outreach has a simple problem: a stranger has no reason to read a long pitch. A useful first message makes the connection relevant, easy to understand, and easy to decline. These examples are starting points for thoughtful B2B outreach—not a reason to send the same message to everyone.

## A practical framework

Before writing, answer three questions:

1. **Why this person?** Point to a role, post, company change, or other detail you can verify.
2. **Why this topic?** Connect that detail to a problem your recipient may recognize.
3. **What is the smallest next step?** Ask one low-effort question, or offer something useful without demanding a meeting.

A compact message often follows this pattern:

> **Context** + **relevant observation** + **small question**

Keep the opening specific and the ask proportionate. A connection request is not the place for a full product overview, a list of credentials, and a calendar link.

## Four templates worth adapting

Replace the bracketed text with accurate details. If you cannot fill in a detail honestly, leave it out.

### 1. Relevant post

> Hi [Name] — I saw your post about [specific topic]. Your point about [specific detail] stood out. I work with [type of team] on [related problem]; curious, is [challenge] something your team is looking at this quarter?

**Why it works:** It starts with something the recipient chose to share and asks a question that can be answered briefly. Avoid pretending a post resonated if you have not read it.

### 2. Role-based observation

> Hi [Name] — I noticed you lead [function] at [Company]. Teams at that stage sometimes run into [specific operational friction]. Is that on your radar, or is another priority taking precedence right now?

**Why it works:** It links the person’s role to a plausible issue without claiming to know their internal situation. “Sometimes” and the easy out keep the message from sounding like a diagnosis.

### 3. Shared connection or context

> Hi [Name] — [Mutual contact / event / group] brought me to your profile. I’m researching how [role or industry] teams handle [specific workflow]. Would you be open to sharing how you approach it? No pitch needed—I’m comparing notes.

**Why it works:** It explains why you are reaching out and makes the purpose clear. Only mention a mutual contact or event when the connection is real, and do not imply an endorsement you were not given.

### 4. Useful resource, no meeting ask

> Hi [Name] — You mentioned [relevant challenge] in [post or conversation]. I put together a short [checklist / example / guide] on [specific outcome]. Want me to send it here? If it’s not relevant, no worries.

**Why it works:** The offer is concrete, and permission comes before sending a link or attachment. Make sure the resource directly addresses the stated topic rather than serving as a disguised sales brochure.

## Follow-up without pressure

If there is no reply, one brief follow-up can be reasonable. Give it a few business days, add context or a useful detail, and make it easy to close the loop.

> Hi [Name] — One quick follow-up in case this got buried. I thought [specific point] might be useful because [brief reason]. If it’s not a priority, I’ll leave it here.

Do not treat silence as consent to keep messaging. Repeated nudges, guilt, or “just bumping this” messages add little value and can damage trust. Respect a clear no, and stop if the person asks you to.

## A quick editing checklist

Before sending, check that:

- The recipient’s name, role, company, and referenced detail are correct.
- The message is about one relevant topic, not several offers at once.
- The first line gives a real reason for contacting this person.
- The ask can be answered easily and does not presume interest.
- The message sounds natural when read aloud and contains no inflated claims.
- You are following LinkedIn’s rules and any applicable privacy and outreach requirements.

Personalization is not adding a name token. It is showing, in one accurate detail, why this message belongs with this person. Start with a small, relevant conversation; let the recipient decide whether it should continue.
